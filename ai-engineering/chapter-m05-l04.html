Evaluation & Experimentation · Lesson 4

Experiment Design, A/B Tests & Statistical Reasoning

A new assistant scores 83% instead of 80%. Is that a real improvement, random variation, or a logging bug? And even if it is real, is it large enough to justify the extra latency and cost?

What you will learn

Write an experiment contract, choose the right randomization unit, distinguish paired offline evaluation from online A/B testing, interpret uncertainty, plan sample size, and make a launch decision that respects data integrity and guardrails.

Continue from LLM judges and human calibration. A measured lift is only as trustworthy as the experiment and the evaluator used to measure it.

1. Decide what would change your mind

We want to replace our support assistant's retrieval configuration. The hypothesis is that the new version helps more users complete their support task without unsupported instructions. Before collecting outcomes, write down exactly what success means and which changes would block a rollout.

Contract itemConcrete example
Population and eligibilityAuthorized support users entering the experiment during a fixed enrollment window; exclusions specified before assignment.
TreatmentOne frozen retrieval change; same answer model, rubric, permissions and logging.
Primary outcomeOne binary result per enrolled user: successful completion of their first eligible support task within a fixed follow-up window.
DenominatorAll enrolled, assigned users. Timeouts and abstentions do not silently disappear; missing outcome telemetry is investigated.
Required improvementFor this lesson, the interval for B minus A must lie entirely above +1 percentage point.
GuardrailsNo observed permission violation; latency and cost within declared budgets; risk slices reviewed.
Analysis and stoppingPredeclared sample size, duration, confidence method, multiplicity policy and final analysis time; safety monitoring throughout.

A higher answered-item accuracy could come from refusing more difficult questions. A user-level task outcome includes failures to serve, while grounding and abstention remain diagnostic metrics. Do not redefine “eligible” after seeing which version performed better.

This chapter's thresholds and counts are teaching choices, not recommended production targets. A real success measure must be validated against the task and measured consistently across variants.

2. Separate paired offline tests from online A/B tests

Offline paired evaluation: run A and B on the same frozen questions and evidence. Compare each question's change, not just two unrelated averages. For binary outcomes, count the discordant pairs: questions where only B succeeds and questions where only A succeeds. A paired test or a paired bootstrap can use that structure. If several questions come from one source or incident, resample the appropriate group rather than treating every question as independent.

Online randomized A/B test: assign eligible units to A or B before outcomes occur. Stable user assignment prevents the same person bouncing between variants. Randomization supports a causal comparison under assumptions about correct assignment, consistent measurement, and limited interference; it does not automatically repair missing logs or shared-state contamination.

Use intent-to-treat analysis to compare assigned groups under the predefined denominator. Filtering afterward to “users who clicked the new feature” can select on treatment effects and bias the comparison. Triggered analyses require a defensible eligibility definition and equivalent exposure logging in both groups.

A shadow run can reveal latency, cost and answer differences without exposing users to B. It cannot establish the causal effect of B on user behavior, because users did not experience it. Offline gains, shadow results and online outcomes answer complementary questions.

3. Count independent units, not log lines

The randomization unit is what you assign, such as a user or tenant. The analysis unit determines how outcomes and uncertainty are calculated. If users are randomized, their repeated requests are correlated. Treating twenty requests from one user as twenty unrelated people can make an interval much too narrow.

Our experiment uses a single first-task outcome per user, making the simple independent-user calculation coherent. For request-level or ratio metrics, use an analysis that respects clustering, such as an appropriate user-level bootstrap, cluster-aware estimator or suitable delta method. Do not assume the independent two-proportion formula below applies to every metric.

If colleagues share assistant answers, caches or workflow state, individual treatment assignments can interfere. Tenant-level randomization may reduce that spillover, but the number of independent tenants can be far smaller than the number of users. With few clusters, a simple user-level calculation is especially misleading. Stable assignment also needs to survive identifier changes and service retries.

Before launch, run an A/A test where both labels receive the same behavior. Inspect assignment ratios, logging completeness and metric behavior. An A/A pass is useful evidence about instrumentation; it cannot prove every future experiment is valid.

4. Read the effect and its uncertainty together

At A=80% and B=83%, the estimated difference is +3 percentage points. The relative lift is (83 − 80) / 80 = 3.75%. Those are different units. Neither tells us how much sampling variation remains.

A confidence interval comes from a procedure designed to cover the fixed population effect at a stated frequency across repeated experiments, under its assumptions. A 95% frequentist interval does not mean there is a 95% probability that this fixed effect lies inside the particular observed interval.

For sufficiently large independent binary samples, the lab illustrates an unpooled normal approximation:

pA = successes_A / users_A
pB = successes_B / users_B
effect = pB - pA
SE = sqrt(pA*(1-pA)/nA + pB*(1-pB)/nB)
95% interval ≈ effect ± 1.96*SE

At 2,000 users per arm and the fixture's observed rates, this gives about +0.60 to +5.40 percentage points. It excludes zero, yet does not establish an improvement above the declared +1-point requirement. A stricter practical-benefit rule can remain inconclusive even when a conventional zero-effect test would find evidence of improvement.

The Wald interval is an educational approximation and can have poor coverage, especially near boundaries. This lab returns no normal interval if any arm has fewer than ten successes or ten failures; that screen is not a guarantee of accuracy. Small counts, rare harms, dependence and ratio metrics need a better-suited method. Intervals here are not adjusted for repeated looks or multiple hypotheses.

A p-value is a tail probability under a specified null hypothesis and statistical model, not the probability that the null is true or the chance the result occurred “by luck.” A small p-value alone says little about practical value. Our treatment-effect simulator emphasizes intervals and a predefined margin; its separate allocation p-value is a data-integrity screen, not evidence of treatment benefit.

Operational summaries are fixed observed fixture values, not statistical bounds on latency or cost. Change these controls to learn the arithmetic, not to search for favorable settings after seeing real data.

5. Plan power before collecting outcomes

Minimum detectable effect (MDE) is the effect size a specified test is designed to detect with a chosen power and sample size. Power is the probability the procedure rejects its null when the specified alternative is true. MDE is not the smallest improvement you might happen to observe, and power is not a post-experiment probability that the result is correct.

A simplified planning approximation for two equal independent arms with binary outcomes is:

n per arm ≈ 2 * (z_(1-alpha/2) + z_power)^2 * p*(1-p) / MDE^2
For p=0.8, two-sided alpha=0.05, power=0.8, MDE=0.03:
n ≈ 2,791 users per arm

This heuristic uses a common baseline variance and tests an effect against zero. It is not a power calculation for the simulator's stricter requirement that the whole interval exceed +1 point. Plan that test against its actual null margin and meaningful alternative. Real sizing also needs allocation ratio, clustering, missing outcomes, follow-up and the chosen inferential method.

Halving the target effect approximately quadruples the sample requirement. More confidence or power also costs observations. Estimate calendar time from eligible unique units and required follow-up, not raw QPS. Include relevant day-of-week cycles and avoid declaring completion before delayed outcomes mature.

6. Do not let the stopping rule manufacture a winner

With a fixed-horizon plan, choose sample size and duration in advance and perform the primary inferential analysis at the declared endpoint. Repeatedly checking an ordinary 95% interval and stopping as soon as it looks favorable changes the procedure and can increase false discoveries.

You can monitor operational health and stop for safety. If the product requires continuous efficacy decisions, use a suitable predeclared sequential method, alpha-spending plan or always-valid procedure. Do not retrofit that justification after opportunistic stopping.

Testing many prompts, models, metrics and slices creates another selection problem. Declare the primary comparison, account for the planned family of confirmatory tests, and label exploratory findings as exploratory. A promising slice discovered after the fact deserves an independent confirmation experiment. Keeping a final holdout untouched helps prevent repeated offline tuning from manufacturing apparent gains.

7. Verify integrity and risk before the launch decision

Sample ratio mismatch (SRM) asks whether observed assignment counts are unexpectedly far from the planned allocation. Our lab uses a Pearson chi-square approximation for a 1:1 plan, with a predeclared screening threshold of 0.001. A detected mismatch calls for investigation of assignment, redirects, eligibility and lost events. It is not repaired by deleting enough rows to make the groups equal. A nonsignificant screen does not prove instrumentation is healthy.

Do not confuse “we did not detect harm” with “we established acceptable risk.” With zero observed violations among n independent users, a one-sided 95% binomial upper bound on the per-user violation probability is 1 − 0.05^(1/n). For 200 users it is about 1.49%; for large n the familiar 3/n approximation is close. The calculation assumes a fixed sample size, reliable violation detection and comparable independent units; missed events defeat the conclusion.

Permission enforcement remains a deterministic boundary even when observed violations are zero. For statistical safety guardrails, define acceptable harm margins and sufficiently powered non-inferiority or risk-bound analyses. “No significant difference” from a tiny sample does not establish non-inferiority.

Finally inspect paired offline regressions, critical slices, evaluator drift, latency, cost, indexing freshness and rollback readiness. A nominally positive average can conceal rare-code failures or tenant-specific harm. Traffic mix can also move aggregates while within-slice behavior differs, so keep denominators and segment counts visible.

Expected reasoning

The distribution of discordant pairs matters, not just net wins. Repeated copies add no independent users, so the valid uncertainty must stay unchanged. Non-inferiority needs an appropriate one-sided bound relative to the declared harm margin, enough observations, and reliable measurement. An underpowered test that fails to detect harm is inconclusive.

Interview-ready explanations

Mid-level · “How would you compare two assistant versions?”

“I'd start with the same labeled questions offline so I can see which cases improve and which regress. For an online test, I'd define one primary outcome, choose a stable assignment unit, and keep the versions and evaluator fixed. I'd decide the sample size and stopping rule before looking at results. Then I'd report the effect with an interval, not just the average or a p-value. I'd also check data quality, safety, latency and cost. A small positive lift wouldn't be enough if the interval is wide or a guardrail gets worse.”

Senior-level · “The new model improves accuracy by three points. Would you ship?”

“First I'd check what that number means: the population, denominator, evaluator and unit of randomization. If users were assigned but requests were analyzed as independent, the uncertainty could be badly understated. I'd inspect allocation and telemetry, then compare the effect interval with a predefined practical-benefit threshold. I'd distinguish an underpowered result from evidence of no useful gain. For offline work I'd preserve paired questions; online I'd use an analysis appropriate to assignment and any clustering or spillover. I'd also check the stopping rule and multiple comparisons so we aren't selecting a lucky winner. Only after the quality evidence and risk guardrails hold would I move to a guarded rollout with rollback triggers. Zero observed permission violations would never replace permission enforcement.”
