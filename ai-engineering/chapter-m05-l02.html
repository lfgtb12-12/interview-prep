Evaluation & Experimentation · Lesson 2

Retrieval and Grounded-Answer Evaluation

The assistant found the right runbook, gave two correct instructions, and attached two citations. Is the answer good? Perhaps. We still need to ask whether it found all the evidence, used that evidence, cited the right passages, and included every safety-critical instruction.

What you will learn

Measure whether useful evidence reaches the model, whether claims follow from that evidence, and whether the answer satisfies the user's request. Calculate recall@k, reciprocal rank and nDCG; distinguish faithfulness from completeness; and evaluate abstention without rewarding an assistant that refuses everything.

Prerequisites: evaluation foundations and RAG evaluation and operations. M04 introduced these measurements in a pipeline; this lesson builds an explicit scoring contract with edge cases, denominators and controlled comparisons.

1. Start with the question and the evidence

Our internal support assistant receives: “PAY-503 timed out. What should I check, and can I retry the charge?” The reviewed answer must include two facts: check payment status by the original request ID, and do not blindly retry an unknown outcome; reconcile it first. This is a fictional runbook used to teach evaluation, not payment-system advice.

PassageReviewed contentRelevance grade
A · PAY-503 statusQuery payment status using the original request ID.3 · directly resolves the diagnostic step
B · reconciliation policyDo not blindly retry an unknown payment; reconcile first.2 · required safety evidence
C · generic retriesRetry transient failures in a read-only health check.0 · different operation
D · dashboard guideChange the dashboard theme.0 · unrelated

These relevance judgments, often called qrels, label unique passage IDs for this question. Grades are human choices under a rubric, not embedding similarities or probabilities. Grade 2 does not mean B is optional: a relevance grade measures usefulness, while the answer checklist marks mandatory facts separately.

Freeze the query, document revisions, authorization snapshot, judged passages and required facts before comparing versions. An alternative valid passage should receive credit after review. If near-duplicate chunks repeat one fact, passage recall can exaggerate coverage: deduplicate or also measure required evidence units. Never count an unauthorized source as evidence the assistant ought to retrieve.

2. Did retrieval find enough evidence?

Suppose the ranking is C, A, B, D and only the first two passages reach the next stage. We found A but missed B. The answer may sound plausible while omitting the rule that prevents a dangerous retry.

MeasurementPlain-English questionExact contract in this lesson
Recall@kHow much relevant evidence did we find?Unique relevant passages in top k ÷ all judged relevant passages. Here: 1/2 = 0.5.
RR@kHow soon did the first useful result appear?1 ÷ rank of first relevant passage within k; 0 if none. Here: 1/2 = 0.5.
MRR@kAcross questions, how early is the first useful result?Mean RR@k over eligible questions, including misses as 0. It is not the RR of one question.
nDCG@kDid the most useful results come first?Discounted graded gain ÷ gain from the ideal top-k ranking.

MRR can reach 1 when A is first even though B never appears. It is useful for first-result tasks but insufficient for questions needing several facts. Recall ignores order within top k, while nDCG rewards better ordering. Report the cutoff: full-ranking MRR and MRR@2 answer different questions.

nDCG, one step at a time

Give a grade-g result a gain of 2^g − 1, then divide by log2(rank + 1), using ranks starting at 1. Add those contributions to get DCG. Compute the best possible DCG from all judged passages sorted by grade, at the same cutoff, and divide actual by ideal. We use exponential gain; other benchmarks may use linear gain, so document your convention.

Ranking C, A at k=2:
DCG  = 0/log2(2) + 7/log2(3) = 4.417
Ideal A, B:
IDCG = 7/log2(2) + 3/log2(3) = 8.893
nDCG@2 = 4.417 / 8.893 ≈ 0.497

The ideal denominator comes from the entire judged set, including relevant passages the system missed. Constructing the ideal from returned results would reward missing evidence. Increasing k may improve recall while lowering the proportion of relevant context, adding latency and wasting tokens; it cannot recover a passage absent from the candidate list.

3. Did the evidence survive context packing?

Track candidate retrieval, reranking and final prompt context separately. A retriever can find B at rank 2, then a context budget or faulty deduplicator can drop it. Candidate recall remains high while final-context recall falls. The fix belongs to context construction, not necessarily embeddings.

Context relevance asks how much of the supplied context helps answer the question. Our simulator uses a deliberately simple passage-level proxy: relevant supplied passages ÷ all supplied passages. Some evaluators judge sentences or token spans instead; their scores are not interchangeable. A long passage with one useful sentence can earn full credit in our proxy while still wasting most tokens.

Relevant does not mean sufficient. A context containing only A is 100% relevant but covers only half the required passages. Record both relevance and recall, plus the actual packed text, source revision and any truncation boundaries.

4. Is the answer supported, properly cited and complete?

Break the response into small factual claims. Judge whether each follows from the supplied evidence, whether its attached citation supports it, and whether the answer includes the independently reviewed required facts. Do not infer support from overlapping keywords: “retry health checks” does not support “retry a charge.”

ScoreDenominator and meaningWhat it can miss
FaithfulnessClaims supported by final context ÷ all emitted factual claims.A faithful answer can omit the safety rule or repeat a stale source.
Citation precisionSupporting claim-citation links ÷ all emitted claim-citation links.One correct citation can hide many uncited claims.
Citation coverageClaims with at least one supporting attached citation ÷ all emitted claims.Every claim can be cited while required facts are absent.
CompletenessDistinct required facts correctly expressed ÷ all required facts.A complete answer can also add unsupported or harmful advice.

In this lab, a valid citation must refer to a supplied passage that individually entails its associated claim. A wrong citation can score 0 precision even if another passage in context supports the claim, leaving faithfulness at 1. Claims needing several passages jointly require evidence-set annotations; this simple single-passage scorer cannot judge them.

We judge correctness separately from grounding. “Check status” can be a correct fact learned from memory while unsupported by today's context. Conversely, an answer can faithfully quote an outdated policy and be wrong for the current task. Completeness here uses independently judged correct facts, not merely words present in the answer.

Metric names vary. Our link-based citation precision is an explicit teaching convention, not an exact reproduction of ALCE's attribution protocol. Use a named rubric and version when comparing experiments. Automatic entailment models and LLM judges approximate semantic judgments; a deterministic calculation over reviewed labels does not automatically discover truth.

5. Measure correct abstention without rewarding silence

Label whether the request is answerable from the current authorized corpus before running retrieval. Also record whether this particular retrieved context is sufficient. If the corpus contains A and B but retrieval misses them, abstaining is safer than inventing a retry policy, yet the end-to-end system still failed to serve an answerable request.

Correct-abstention rate here is abstained unanswerable requests ÷ all unanswerable requests. False-abstention rate is abstained answerable requests ÷ all answerable requests. For four labeled cases—two answerable with one abstention, two unanswerable with one abstention—both rates are 1/2. Always pair them with answer quality and coverage. Refusing everything gives 100% correct abstention and 100% false abstention.

An outage is not evidence that a question is unanswerable. Report retrieval unavailable, permission denial and genuinely insufficient authorized evidence as distinct outcomes. Evaluate partial answers under an explicit product policy: a transparent partial answer may be useful, but it should not receive full completeness credit.

Undefined values: with no relevant judged passages, recall, RR and nDCG are N/A in this lesson; evaluate those requests in the unanswerable slice. With relevant passages but zero hits, recall and RR are 0. With no emitted claims, faithfulness and citation scores are N/A, not 1. With claims but no citations, citation precision is N/A and citation coverage is 0. Publish eligible counts so exclusions cannot hide regressions.

6. Turn a failed score into an engineering decision

Observed patternInspect firstControlled comparison
Low candidate recallIngestion, ACL filters, exact-code matching, query interpretationSame questions and corpus; compare lexical/hybrid retrieval.
High candidate recall, low context recallReranker, token budget, truncation, duplicate removalFreeze candidates; compare packed contexts.
Sufficient context, low faithfulnessPrompt, model, claim verificationSupply reviewed evidence to both generators.
High faithfulness, low completenessRequired-fact coverage, question decompositionCheck whether facts are absent from context or ignored.
Good grounding, poor citation precisionClaim-to-source attribution and citation renderingCompare exact cited spans with each atomic claim.
High false abstentionMissing evidence versus overly strict answer policySeparate insufficient context from sufficient-context refusal.

For a generation experiment, freeze retrieval and feed both generators the same reviewed context. For a retrieval experiment, freeze the generator and compare both intermediate metrics and end-to-end answers. An oracle-context experiment supplies reviewed sufficient evidence: an improvement suggests an evidence-delivery bottleneck, but does not prove the generator works on realistic noisy context. Several stages can fail at once; track contributing failures as well as the first broken stage.

Keep paired per-query results, not just averages. Report macro means over eligible questions and separate claim-level micro rates if needed: long answers otherwise dominate. Include denominators, slice counts, cost, latency and paired uncertainty estimates. Pool and adjudicate top results from competing retrievers so unjudged new sources are not silently treated as irrelevant. Tune on development data; preserve an untouched final set.

Expected reasoning for the extensions

A duplicate status passage adds a relevant passage without adding a new fact, so passage recall and fact coverage can move differently. For joint support, annotate alternative sufficient evidence sets and require a complete set to be present; citation credit must also account for joint entailment. The uncited response has undefined citation precision and zero citation coverage. An outage needs its own operational category. A sufficient-context refusal is a generation-policy failure and an end-to-end false abstention.

Interview-ready explanations

Mid-level · “How would you evaluate a RAG assistant?”

“I'd test retrieval and the final answer separately. For each question, I'd label the passages we should find and the facts a good answer needs. Recall@k tells me whether enough evidence was retrieved; reciprocal rank and nDCG help me judge the order. Then I'd check whether each answer claim is supported, whether its citation actually backs it up, and whether any required facts are missing. I'd include questions where the right response is to abstain. If a result is bad, I'd trace where the evidence was lost before changing the model.”

Senior-level · “Our nDCG improved. Can we ship?”

“That's encouraging, but I'd want to know whether users get better answers. I'd compare both versions on the same versioned questions, authorized corpus and reviewed labels. I'd inspect candidate recall, the context that actually reached the model, claim support, citation attribution and completeness as separate measurements. A ranking improvement can still drop a mandatory safety fact during context packing. I'd also check answerable and unanswerable slices so a higher score isn't coming from more refusals. For diagnosis I'd freeze context when comparing generators, and try reviewed oracle context to see how much of the failure comes from evidence delivery. I'd report paired results, uncertainty, risky-slice regressions, latency and cost before a guarded rollout. Permission violations would remain hard failures, regardless of nDCG.”

Sources and scoring conventions

The Stanford information retrieval textbook explains ranked retrieval evaluation. RAGAs (Es et al., 2024) motivates evaluating retrieval and generation dimensions separately. ALCE (Gao et al., 2023) evaluates generated answers and citation quality. This chapter's fixtures, relevance proxy, completeness checklist and link-based citation arithmetic are explicitly defined teaching choices; scores should not be presented as reproductions of these benchmarks.
